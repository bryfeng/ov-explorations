A neutral, content-blind root of trust.

Independent systems can share one record of what happened and in what order, without adopting each other's rules.

The record sits underneath; everything that carries meaning stays above it, in the systems themselves. Value, here, means records of economically consequential events. In every figure, the dashed line is what is proposed.

01 · Why a shared record is needed

Order across systems is agreed pair by pair.

When one event touches two systems, each records it under its own rules, in its own order. To establish that A happened before B across them, someone fetches both records and reconciles them. That work is done once per pair, and pairs grow faster than systems: five systems have ten, fifty have 1,225.

A shared record of order would let each system do that work once, against one reference. Validation and meaning do not move; only the work of agreeing on order does.

02 · The record underneath

Only a fingerprint goes down.

A fingerprint is a cryptographic commitment to an event, made by the system that owns it, in that system's own format. The event stays in the system. What a fingerprint reveals is the system's choice, and part of how records are later disclosed selectively.

Open Value proposes an ordering service: a record that puts fingerprints in one order and is meant to keep them available. It is neutral in two senses: content-blind, so it carries no meaning of its own; and belonging to no system's rules, so using it is not adopting anyone else's. Events are not executed, validated or interpreted there. State is always derived from events, above the record.

Order is not time. A record is not proof. Ordered records of commitments already exist for single domains under named operators; what is proposed differs in scope: one order across unrelated systems, with no privileged operator. Whether that is achievable is the research question.

03 · Who does what

Fingerprints go down. A position comes back. Readers check later.

As proposed: a system submits a fingerprint of an event it has already recorded under its own rules, receives a position in the order, and stores that position next to its own record. That is the integration footprint. A reader — an auditor, a regulator, a counterparty, or the same system later — obtains the disclosed event through whatever access exists today, recomputes the fingerprint and checks it against the record. The record adds no new channel; disclosure and retention stay with the system under its existing law.

Content never crosses down. No rule flows up. A reversal is a new event in the order, not an edit. Whether shared conventions form between systems and the record, one layer or several, is open.

04 · One example · illustrative

A cash leg and an asset leg in different systems.

A bank holds the cash leg of a trade; a registry holds the asset leg. Each submits a fingerprint of its leg as it settles and keeps the position it receives. Months later an auditor with both disclosed records shows that the cash fingerprint sits before the asset fingerprint in one order, without either system adopting the other's rules.

Sequence, not clock time. Inclusion, not validity. The same shape applies to a vehicle's history across insurers and registries, to an ACH reversal recorded after the original, and to value moving between an airline and a telco. All illustrative; none is a deployment.

05 · What is not known

The ordering service is proposed, not built. Six questions are open.

  1. 01
    OrderingWho decides the order, and how do they agree without a coin to stake? Tokenless and permissionless operation are objectives of this question, not results.
  2. 02
    IdentityWhat stops one actor from pretending to be a million?
  3. 03
    AvailabilityHow does ordered data stay available when no one stores all of it?
  4. 04
    LongevityHow does data survive long term when no one is paid to keep it?
  5. 05
    Proof across applicationsHow does an outsider prove A happened before B across unrelated applications?
  6. 06
    BehaviourWhat makes participants behave with no coin to lose?

A shared record is necessary for independent systems to agree on order; it is not sufficient.

06 · Open call for proposals

Work on one of the six questions.

Open Value is a Swiss Verein, a public-benefit association, run as an open research initiative. It invites pre-proposals on any of the six questions, from individuals and groups, in any discipline that bears on them: cryptography, distributed systems, economics, law, enterprise infrastructure.

Pre-proposals are short: the question, the approach, what a result would look like, and who is doing the work.
